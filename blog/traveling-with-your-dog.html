Traveling With Your Dog: The Documents You Actually Need

There's a real pet passport (the EU has one), and then there's the pile of paperwork everyone else improvises. Here's the pile, organised.

Dog travel paperwork feels chaotic because three different parties want proof of three different things: governments care about rabies, airlines care about health and crate standards, and hotels/kennels care about vaccine currency. Sort your documents into those three buckets and every trip becomes the same checklist.

The universal three

Wherever you're going, you will at some point be asked for:

  1. Rabies vaccination certificate — the actual signed certificate with dates, not the little tag on the collar. Most jurisdictions require the shot to be at least 21–30 days old and still current on arrival day.
  2. Microchip number — an ISO 11784/11785 chip is the international standard. The chip must be implanted before the rabies shot for the vaccine to count in most countries (order matters, annoyingly).
  3. A recent health certificate — a vet's signed statement that your dog is fit to travel, usually dated within 10 days of departure for flights.

Domestic road trips

Legally light: carry rabies proof and your dog's licence. Practically: bring vaccine records anyway, because hotels and any boarding you improvise mid-trip will ask for bordetella and DHPP too. A digital copy on your phone covers 99% of it — the full list of what belongs in that file is in our health records guide.

Flying (domestic or international)

  • Airline health certificate: almost every carrier wants a vet certificate issued within 10 days of the flight — for each direction on longer trips.
  • Crate compliance: IATA-standard crate for cargo; airline-specific dimensions for in-cabin. Book early — most flights cap in-cabin pets at 2–4 total.
  • Flat-faced breeds: many airlines refuse pugs, bulldogs and frenchies in cargo on safety grounds. Check before booking anything.

International: the big three systems

The EU pet passport

The genuine article: a booklet issued by EU vets recording microchip, rabies shots and treatments, valid for life across the EU while vaccines stay current. Non-EU dogs enter on an EU animal health certificate instead (valid 4 months per trip). Once inside, you can get a real passport from any EU vet — worth it if you'll return.

The UK, Australia, Japan and other island rules

Rabies-free islands are strict: expect rabies titer tests (a blood test proving antibody levels), waiting periods measured in months, tapeworm treatments on a timed window, and in Australia's case, managed quarantine. These trips are planned in quarters, not weeks — start six months out.

The US

Entry requirements tightened in recent years: dogs need to be microchipped, at least six months old, and arrive with a CDC dog import form; requirements step up sharply if the dog has been in a high-risk rabies country in the past six months. Check the CDC's current rules when you book, not when you pack.

Keep the originals, carry the copies

Border agents want paper; everyone else accepts a phone screen. Keep a digital set of everything — certificates, chip number, insurance — somewhere structured, so the 6am check-in question ("proof of rabies?") is a two-tap answer.
