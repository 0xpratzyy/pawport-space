Dog Training Journal: What to Log and the Best App for It

Trainers keep logs because memory lies. Here is the smallest journal that still changes behaviour, and how to keep it going past week two.

Every professional trainer keeps a log, and almost no owner does. The reason is not discipline. It is that most training journals ask for too much: a form with twelve fields, a rating scale, a mood. By the third session the form wins and the journal stops. The version that survives is one line per session and one honest look back per week. This guide is that version, plus the app question at the end.

Why a training log changes the dog, not just the owner

Dogs learn in reps, and progress is invisible day to day. A recall that works 4 times out of 10 on Monday and 6 out of 10 on Friday feels like nothing while it is happening; written down, it is a 50 percent improvement in a week and a reason to keep going. The log also catches the thing every owner does by accident: raising difficulty too fast. If the notes say "garden: 9/10, park: 2/10", the fix is obvious (add a middle step, a quiet street), and without the notes it reads as "my dog is stubborn".

What to log after each session

Seven things, and they fit in one line.

FieldExampleWhy it matters
Date and lengthTue, 6 minShort sessions win; the log proves it
CuerecallOne cue per session, mostly
Environmentgarden, cat next doorDistraction is the real variable
Reps and hits10 reps, 7 hitsThe only number you will chart
Rewardcheese, then kibbleExplains a sudden dip
Distance or duration15 mFor recall, stay, loose leash
Next timesame spot, 20 mThe plan, written while you remember

A real entry looks like this: "Tue 6 min · recall · park edge, joggers · 10/7 · cheese · 15 m · next: 20 m, quieter hour." That is the whole journal. If you have thirty seconds, write it. If you have five minutes, add what the dog's body did on the misses (looked away first, froze, went to sniff), because that is the diagnostic.

The weekly review, in three questions

Once a week, read the lines back and answer three things. Which cue moved? Which one did not move in two weeks (change the method, not the reps)? Where did the environment jump too far (a 9/10 followed by a 2/10 means a missing step, not a bad dog)? Write the three answers under the week. That is the part most owners skip, and it is where the value is.

Puppies, rescues and seniors log differently

Puppies: log socialisation exposures alongside cues, one line per new thing (bus, vacuum, child on a scooter) with a calm/worried note. The window closes around 14 weeks; the log tells you what you have covered. Pair it with the socialisation guide and the vaccine schedule so park sessions start on the right week.

Rescues: add a trigger column. Which things caused a reaction, at what distance, and what happened next. Over a month the distances shrink and the log becomes the story you tell the behaviourist.

Seniors: training is maintenance and enrichment. Log the cue and the energy afterwards; a dog that used to do ten reps and now tires at four is data for the vet, not a training problem. See senior dog care.

Paper, notes app, or a dog app?

Paper works until it gets lost. The phone's notes app works until you want to see a trend. A spreadsheet works if you are the kind of person who opens spreadsheets on a Tuesday evening, which is rare. The honest comparison, the same one we make in our roundup of dog journal apps:

  • Dedicated training apps (per-cue statistics, timers, trainer sharing) are the right pick if you compete, work with a behaviourist weekly, or have a reactive dog with a structured plan. They are the wrong pick for most families because the training log lives apart from everything else about the dog and gets abandoned.
  • Pawport treats a training day the way it treats a walk or a bath: a stamped page in the passport with a photo, the session line as the caption, and the date. Recall practice sits next to the vet visit and the beach day, so the journal is something you actually open. Family members add their sessions to the same book. What it does not do is per-cue charts; the weekly review is yours to write.
  • A plain notebook is still the best option for the first two weeks if you are not sure you will keep it up. Move to an app once the habit exists.

A two-week starter template

Week one: one cue (recall or loose leash), six sessions of five minutes, all at home or in the garden. Log the line every time. Week two: same cue, three sessions at home, three in one slightly harder place, and the first weekly review. If the hit rate in the harder place is below 6 out of 10, week three repeats week two. Progress in dog training is boring on purpose.

Common questions

How often should I write in a dog training journal?

After every session, even if it is one line. Three sessions a week with a one-line note beats one long entry on Sunday, because the useful detail (what the dog did on the third rep) is gone by evening.

What should a dog training log include?

Date, cue, environment, number of reps, success rate, the reward used, and one sentence on what to change next time. Add distance, duration and distraction level for recall and loose-leash work.

Is there an app for logging dog activity and training together?

Pawport files walks, training days, baths and vet visits as stamped pages in one passport, with the training note as the caption. A dedicated training app is better only if you need per-cue statistics.
