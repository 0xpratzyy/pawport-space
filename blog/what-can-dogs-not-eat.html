What Can Dogs Not Eat?

Not everything on the scary lists is equally scary. Here's the toxic-food table ranked by real danger, so you know what's a vet-now moment and what's a watch-and-wait.

Every dog parent eventually plays a frantic round of "he ate WHAT?", usually at 9pm. The internet's answer is a hundred identical lists where a grape and a slice of avocado look equally terrifying. They are not. Danger is a function of what, how much, and how big your dog is.

The danger table

FoodDangerWhat you need to know
Xylitol (sugar-free gum, some peanut butters)ExtremeTiny amounts crash blood sugar within 30–60 min. Vet immediately, always.
Grapes & raisinsExtremeKidney failure; no established safe dose, unpredictable per dog. Vet now, even for one or two.
Dark chocolate & cocoaHighDanger scales with darkness and dog size. Baking chocolate is the emergency tier; a lab licking a milk-chocolate smear is usually a phone call, not a crisis.
Onions, garlic, leeksHighDamage red blood cells; danger accumulates over days. Concentrated forms (onion soup mix, garlic powder) are worse than a stray cooked slice.
Macadamia nutsHighWeakness, tremors, wobbliness within 12 hours. Usually recoverable, still call the vet.
Alcohol & raw yeast doughHighDough rises and ferments in the stomach, mechanical and alcohol emergency at once.
Cooked bonesModerate–HighSplinter risk, especially poultry. Raw meaty bones are a different (supervised) conversation.
Corn cobsModerate–HighNot toxic, a classic intestinal blockage. Watch for vomiting and no stool.
AvocadoLow–ModerateFlesh in small amounts is usually fine; the pit (blockage) and skin are the real problems.
DairyLowMost adult dogs are lactose-intolerant. Result: an unpleasant day, not an emergency.

If your dog just ate something on the list

  1. Figure out how much and when. Wrapper forensics count. Weight of dog + amount eaten is the first thing every vet asks.
  2. Call before you drive, your vet, an emergency clinic, or a pet poison hotline. For xylitol or grapes, call while walking to the car.
  3. Don't induce vomiting on your own. With some substances it makes things worse; let a professional make that call.
  4. Write it down, what, how much, when, symptoms, what the vet said. Panic memory is terrible, and next time (there is always a next time) you'll want the record. Filed in the health drawer, ideally.

Safe snacks worth knowing

For balance: carrots, apple slices (no core or seeds), blueberries, plain cooked chicken, green beans, watermelon (no rind), and plain pumpkin are all solid choices, pumpkin is even a vet favourite for digestion. Peanut butter is fine if the label has no xylitol, which is worth checking every single time the brand changes.

The best defence isn't memorising lists, it's counter-management, a good "leave it," and knowing your emergency vet's number before you need it. Put that number in your dog's travel kit too.
