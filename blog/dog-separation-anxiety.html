Dog Separation Anxiety: Signs and a Plan

A chewed shoe is boredom. Panic at the door is something else. Here's how to tell the difference — and the slow, boring training plan that actually fixes it.

Separation anxiety isn't disobedience — it's panic. A dog in genuine distress isn't choosing to howl or destroy the doorframe any more than a person having a panic attack is choosing a racing heart. Which is why punishment does nothing and the fix is gradual, unglamorous, and very effective.

Anxiety or boredom? The diagnosis table

SignBoredomSeparation anxiety
When it happensAny time, alone or notStarts within ~30 min of you leaving
What's destroyedFun things: cushions, toys, remoteExit points: doors, frames, windowsills
VocalisingOccasional, stopsPersistent howling/whining, doesn't settle
Bathroom accidentsRare if trainedCommon even in fully trained dogs
Greeting on returnHappyFrantic, drooling, took ages to settle
Pre-departureIndifferentPacing/panting as you pick up keys

Not sure? Prop a phone against the fruit bowl and record the first 30 minutes alone. Two minutes of footage answers what months of guessing can't.

The plan: graded departures

The core idea: leave for durations so short the panic never starts, and stretch them so slowly the dog barely notices. You're not training obedience — you're rebuilding the belief that you come back.

  1. Kill the departure cues first. Pick up keys, put on shoes, then… sit back down. Repeat until keys mean nothing.
  2. Start absurdly small. Step outside, close the door, come back in 10 seconds. Before the whining starts, not after.
  3. Stretch unevenly. 10s, 30s, 15s, 1 min, 40s, 2 min… predictable ramps teach dogs to count; scattered ones teach calm.
  4. Keep exits and returns boring. No dramatic goodbyes, no jackpot greetings. You leaving is the least interesting event of the day.
  5. Log every session. Duration and result. Progress is invisible day to day and obvious week to week — the log is what keeps you going in week two.

Between sessions, real exercise moves the needle more than any gadget: a properly sniffy walk before your actual departure buys calm that a stuffed toy can't.

What makes it worse

  • Punishment after the fact. The dog connects your anger to your arrival, not the chewed door — making arrivals scarier, which fuels the cycle.
  • "Flooding" — leaving for hours hoping they'll get used to it. Panic practiced is panic strengthened.
  • Getting a second dog as a fix. Sometimes helps, often produces two anxious dogs. Fix the anxiety first.

When to get help

Severe cases — self-injury, escape attempts through windows, hours of panic — deserve a veterinary behaviourist, and sometimes short-term medication to make training possible. That's not failure; it's a ramp. Note episodes and triggers in the health record so the professional starts with data instead of anecdotes.

Log the calm daysEvery uneventful alone-time is a milestone worth a stamp. The streak is the proof it's working.
